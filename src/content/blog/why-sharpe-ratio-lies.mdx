The first number anyone shows you on a quant pitch deck is the Sharpe ratio. It's clean, scale-free, comparable across strategies. And dangerously easy to game.

Three failure modes I've personally walked into:

## 1. Higher Sharpe from shorter histories

Sample Sharpe is a noisy estimate of true Sharpe. Run a coin-flip strategy long enough and you'll see realized Sharpes ranging from -1 to +1.5 just from sample variance. The shorter the backtest, the wider the band. Yet the convention is to report a single number, without a confidence interval.

## 2. Sharpe ignores higher moments

The Sharpe formula assumes returns are roughly Gaussian. They aren't. Strategies with negative skew (sell options, momentum during a regime shift) can post lovely Sharpes for years and then blow up in a single month. The metric never told you about the tail.

## 3. Backtest overfitting inflates Sharpe by construction

Try N strategy parameterizations, pick the best Sharpe, report it. The expected best Sharpe under the null hypothesis (no real edge) grows roughly with √(2 ln N). You will *always* find a "0.8 Sharpe" strategy if you tried 50 of them.

## The fix: Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio

Lopez de Prado's PSR computes the probability that the *true* Sharpe exceeds a benchmark, given the observed Sharpe, sample size, and the higher moments of returns. The formula:

```
PSR(SR*) = Φ( (SR_obs - SR*) · √(n - 1) / √(1 - γ_3·SR_obs + (γ_4 - 1)/4 · SR_obs²) )
```

where γ₃ is skew, γ₄ is kurtosis, n is sample size, and Φ is the standard normal CDF. Plug in your observed Sharpe, the benchmark you want to beat, and you get a probability.

I now report `PSR(0.5)` alongside Sharpe. If it's below 95%, the result isn't statistically distinguishable from "lucky".

## A practical workflow

For [quant-factor-portfolio](/projects/quant-factor-portfolio) my reporting checklist is:

1. **Sharpe.** The field's lingua franca. Required.
2. **PSR(0.5).** Is the result statistically real?
3. **Bootstrap 95% CI on Sharpe.** Visual companion to PSR.
4. **Sortino + Max Drawdown.** Captures asymmetry that Sharpe misses.
5. **Information Ratio vs benchmark.** Answers "did I add value?", not "did I make money?".

It's three more lines of code per backtest. Catches a lot of self-deception.
